01 — The correction

Correction to the earlier analysis

I said the Risk Radar export has no date fields, and that this makes a real forecast track record impossible without invention. That was wrong.

I was looking at the 25 CSV columns. The dates are inside the Triggers text blob, which I had parsed for trigger IDs and completion rates but not to the end of each block. Every one of the 266 trigger blocks carries:

  • Date entered: — a full date, on 266 of 266 blocks, spanning 5 Feb 2024 → 5 Oct 2026
  • Last update: — a full date, also on 266 of 266
  • Completion rate history: — dated, timestamped entries in the form 03-09-2026: 60%
  • Trend: and Trend history: — populated on 165 of 166 blocks

So the picture is more nuanced than either "no dates" or "fully dated":

ClaimReality
Structured date columns in the exportnone Still true. 25 columns, zero timestamps.
Dates anywhere in the datayes In the trigger text, complete on every block.
Dated completion-rate seriespartial 140 of 266 blocks have ≥1 dated entry; only 40 are genuine multi-point series. Median is one point.
A recoverable question open-dateyes Date entered gives you when a trigger entered the radar — an approximate question open date.
A recoverable resolution datepartly Not direct, but Last update bounds it, and for materialised risks the outcome text often carries a date.

This makes the BENCHMARK story better, not worse. The data contains its own audit trail — timestamps on when each trigger entered, when it was last touched, and how its completion rate moved. You do not have to invent resolution infrastructure; you have to excavate it. That is a materially stronger claim, and the sentence for the pitch changes from "the dataset can't support a track record" to "the dataset contains its own audit trail, and nobody has parsed it."

02 — Completion rate behaves like a probability

F2

A second, independent, expert-derived signal — and it works

high impact

I tested whether Completion rate predicts risk materialisation, treating it naively as a probability with no fitting. On the 35 resolved risks that carry trigger data:

ACAPS probabilityAUC .777
Completion rate (max)AUC .745
Completion rate (mean)AUC .741

Brier on the same 35 risks: ACAPS probability 0.1847, raw completion rate 0.2147 — and the completion rate figure is a raw, unfitted value, so a fitted model would close most of the gap.

What this means for you. Completion rate is not noise and not decoration: it is a second predictive signal that tracks the same underlying quantity — the chance the hazard fires. That has two consequences:

  • A1 gets stronger. You now have two expert-derived baselines per trigger — the ACAPS probability rating on the parent risk, and the trigger's own completion rate. Where they disagree, that disagreement is itself an interesting object to put in front of an expert.
  • My Day-2 question narrows but does not vanish. Empirically it behaves like P(trigger fires). Ask ACAPS to confirm the definition anyway — but you can now proceed on the empirical reading without waiting for the reply.

Caveat: n=35, and completion rate is aggregated to risk level here (max and mean across a risk's triggers). Treat as a direction worth stating with its sample size, not a headline number.

03 — ACAPS's own risk matrix does not reproduce the data

F3

78% of rated risks contradict the published Probability × Impact grid

medium impact

docs/risk-methodology.md publishes the exact 5×5 matrix ACAPS uses to derive Risk Level from Probability and Impact. I checked every row in the export against it. 97 of 125 checkable rows match (78%). 28 do not.

ExampleIn the dataPer ACAPS's own matrix
Probability High, Impact 3Risk Level HighMedium
Probability Medium, Impact 5Risk Level MediumHigh
Probability Low, Impact 2Risk Level MediumLow

Two readings. Either the export spans multiple versions of the methodology and the matrix changed, or analysts override the derived level with judgement — which the methodology supports ("analysts use professional judgement and peer review").

Why you should care. Any model that treats Risk Level as a clean function of Probability × Impact will be wrong ~1 time in 5, and more importantly: it tells you ACAPS analysts already override the formula. That is an argument for expert input, and a caution against building scoring on Risk Level alone. Use Probability as the continuous signal; treat Risk Level as a label with noise.

Worth raising with Alexander Harper as a genuine question, not a gotcha: "we noticed 22% of ratings don't follow the published matrix — is that version drift or analyst override?" Either answer tells you something about whether expert judgement is welcome upstream.

04 — Three different rubrics are in circulation

This is the most consequential finding in the audit, and it is easy to miss because each rubric lives in a different place and nobody has reconciled them.

SourceStated criteriaStatus
Junction challenge page
both challenges		 Concept 40 · Prototype 20 · Technology Feasibility 15 · Product Potential 15 · Business Feasibility 5 · Wow Factor 5 The one everyone reads. Presented as authoritative, identical for both tracks.
UNICRI repo — PITCH_GUIDE.md
and repeated in README.md		 Impact on prevention 25 · Adoptability 25 · Creativity 20 · Safety & ethics 20 · Clarity of pitch 10 Completely different weighting. Two independent files agree on it. Almost certainly the live UNICRI rubric.
ACAPS repo — evaluation-criteria.md Seven unweighted dimensions: problem understanding · expert experience · anonymity & trust · feasibility · data use · innovation · presentation Prefaced with "while specific jury weights may vary" — an explicit hedge.

Read this carefully, because it is a strategy problem not a trivia problem. The ACAPS materials never confirm the 40/20/15/15/5/5 weighting — the repo explicitly says weights may vary. The UNICRI materials assert a different rubric with confidence, twice. Either the tracks genuinely use different jury rubrics, or the documents were written at different times and never reconciled.

The only safe strategy is to build and pitch to the union. Every criterion across all three lists, with no gaps:

Working prototype (20% / "feasibility")Runs end to end on real data, with the anonymity gate visibly refusing.

Evidence & impact (15% product / 25% impact)The benchmark number and the measured panel result.
Adoptability (UNICRI 25%)Chad pilot specced, costed, ready in December; ≤20 min/week moderation.
Creativity (20%)The refusal, the receipt, the dissent router — three things nobody else has.
Clarity / presentation (10% / 5%)2:45 rehearsed, opens on a finding about ACAPS.
Business (5%)One sentence. Who signs, what it costs.

And then ask. "Which rubric is the live one for this track, and are the weights in the platform page the ones the jury uses?" is a completely reasonable question at the Friday deep dive, and it costs you nothing.

05 — The UNICRI repo leaks the jury's questions

F5

PITCH_GUIDE.md states the pitch is 3 min + 2 min Q&A — and lists the questions

high impact

Nobody reads past the brief. The guide contains three things that change preparation:

  1. Five minutes, not three. "Your pitch is 3 minutes followed by 2 minutes of Q&A from the jury." A three-minute pitch with no Q&A prep is a pitch that ends badly.
  2. The minute-by-minute structure they want: minute 1 problem and target user (make it concrete with the brief's own example), minute 2 live demo on the mock platform, minute 3 evidence + safe test plan + next steps.
  3. The exact jury questions, written down:
    • "How does this work in a country with different confidentiality rules?"
    • "What stops someone from dismissing the warning?"
    • "How do you avoid habituation on repeated views?"
    • "What data does the platform need to share?"
    • "How do you know it works if you cannot track individuals?"

Note what those five questions are. They are precisely the four UNICRI directions I ranked highest — Honest Router, the Ladder, and Blind Measurement. The organisers have told you what the jury will probe. A team that answers all five in the pitch pre-empts the interrogation; a team that does not will spend its two minutes defending.

For ACAPS, no equivalent guide exists — which means the Q&A is unscripted and you must anticipate it yourself. The five questions translate almost one-for-one: how does this work across countries → multilingual panels; what stops dismissal → what stops an expert ignoring a prompt; habituation → panel fatigue; what data is shared → the anonymity contract; how do you know it works without tracking individuals → the calibration record. Prepare those five answers for the ACAPS jury too.

06 — The two repos are not equally supported

Side by side, the tracks differ in what the organisers have built for you — and that asymmetry should inform which one you pick and how you spend your time.

ProvidedACAPSUNICRIConsequence
Working starter code✗✓UNICRI teams start running; ACAPS teams start from zero.
Submission template✗✓ACAPS teams must invent their own structure.
One-pager / safe-test-plan template✗✓UNICRI pushes you to articulate measurement; ACAPS does not ask.
Pitch guide with jury questions✗✓The single biggest information asymmetry between tracks.
CI workflow + PR template✗✓Signals UNICRI expects code contributions; ACAPS expects analysis.
Starter notebook✓✗ACAPS gives you the analytic starting point instead.
Real dataset with outcomes✓✗The one place ACAPS wins decisively: falsifiable evidence.

Read together: UNICRI is productised — you get scaffolding, a template, and the jury's questions. ACAPS is an analytical problem — you get a dataset and nothing else. This does not change the recommendation (ACAPS still wins on falsifiability), but it sharpens the work: on ACAPS you are not just building the product, you are building the submission apparatus too. Make that an advantage. A repo that contains a validity dossier, a benchmark script, a red-team suite and a written threat model will read as more serious than a filled-in template — precisely because nobody gave you a template.

07 — The organisers' own notebook sets a low bar

F7

The starter notebook ignores everything interesting in the data

opportunity

I read all 23 cells of notebooks/data-exploration.ipynb. It is competent and shallow, and its gaps map exactly onto your advantage:

  • Its parse_triggers() function extracts id, title and description — and truncates the description to 200 characters. It never touches Completion rate, Trend, Date entered, or the history. Everything in Finding 2 and the correction above is invisible to anyone who only runs this notebook.
  • Section 5 says "let's validate the materialisation rates" and then prints the table without any assessment. It does not flag that the Low bucket has n=3.
  • It closes with a "Next Steps" list that maps one-to-one onto the brief: ground truthing, forecasting, Brier scoring, tiered anonymity.
  • It ends with the single most useful operational line in either repo: "Alexander Harper from ACAPS is on site — use that access."

What this tells you. The organisers expect most teams to stop where the notebook stops. If the median submission is built on cell 15's parser — 200-char descriptions, no completion rates, no dates — then arriving with the full 266-block parse including dated histories and a calibration test is a visible, demonstrable step beyond what you were handed. That is exactly what "more than just an idea" is asking for.

08 — A trap in the UNICRI tooling

F8

The supplied traffic generator bakes in a 15× lift that does not exist

trap

tools/generate-traffic.js ships with hard-coded outcome rates per variant:

Variantclicked_helpdismissedcontinuedleft_page
control0.010.250.500.24
deterrent-v10.150.300.350.20
deterrent-v20.180.280.320.22

The control is set at the brief's real-world 1-in-100 figure — and the treatment is set at 15–18%. That is a 15–18× lift, produced by arithmetic someone typed into a fixture file. Any team that runs the generator and screenshots the dashboard is showing you the constant they were given, not a result.

For calibration: the brief's own best real-world evidence is a 1% → ~2% click-through in the Project Intercept scale-up, and 8.7% → 15.7% on the Ahmia dark-web experiment — where the baseline is high-intent users already on a dedicated search engine, not a hobby forum. A credible clearnet forum result is closer to 1% → 1.5–2%, which is exactly the sample-size problem I calculated earlier: you need ~4,600 impressions to detect a doubling, and ~15,500 to detect a 50% lift.

How to use this. Two moves, both cheap. First, don't fall for it — set your synthetic generator's parameters to a defensible effect size and say why. Second, say it out loud in the pitch: "the provided generator assumes a 15× lift; real-world evidence suggests 1.5–2×, which means a small platform needs roughly six weeks before it can detect anything at all — here is how our dashboard behaves in that window." That single sentence demonstrates you read the tooling, understood the evidence base, and refused to flatter yourself. It is worth more than any feature you could add in the same twenty minutes.

10 — New questions for Friday

Replacing the old list, since two of the earlier questions are now partly answered by the data.

For ACAPS — Alexander Harper, on site

  1. "We parsed all 266 trigger blocks, including Date entered, Last update and the dated completion-rate history — most people only use the titles. Was that meant to be available to us?" Frames you as the team that went deeper, and confirms you are not reading a field you were not supposed to have.
  2. "What does Completion rate mean exactly — your confidence the trigger fires, or progress toward it?" Empirically it behaves like a probability (AUC .745), but confirm. Still the highest-value question in the room.
  3. "22% of rated risks don't follow your published Probability × Impact matrix. Version drift, or analyst override?" Genuine curiosity, and the answer tells you whether expert judgement is welcome upstream — which is the premise of your whole project.
  4. "Which rubric is live for this track? The platform page says Concept 40%, the repo says weights may vary." Ask plainly. Nobody will be offended and you may save yourself a misallocated weekend.
  5. "Is there a pitch guide and a one-pager template for ACAPS, like the UNICRI track has?" If not, you have just found your submission format — and the opportunity to set the standard.

For UNICRI — Emma Persson, and the online Q&A with Dr Rahm and Dr Nurmi

  1. "The supplied traffic generator assumes a 15× lift in click-through. Was that chosen for demo clarity, or is it your expectation?" The answer tells you whether the organisers will reward statistical honesty or flashy dashboards. Either way you learn something.
  2. "The Ahmia result was 8.7% → 15.7% on a dark-web search engine. What do you consider a realistic clearnet forum baseline?" Get the number from the people who ran the study, not from a fixture file.

The one thing to take from this audit

The repos contain more than the brief does, and less than they appear to. More: dated trigger histories, a predictive completion-rate field, and — for UNICRI — the jury's actual questions. Less: a settled rubric, a pitch guide for ACAPS, and any tooling for the ACAPS track at all.

The asymmetry is the opportunity. Everyone will read the brief. Almost nobody will read PITCH_GUIDE.md, notice that the folder says submission/, and realise the organisers have quietly told one track what the jury will ask. And nobody will parse the trigger blocks to the end.
