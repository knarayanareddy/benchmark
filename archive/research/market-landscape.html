01

The answer in one page

Before the reports: the market is far more crowded than the briefs imply, and almost entirely empty in the one place that matters. There are mature commercial forecasting platforms selling to governments. There is world-class statistical early warning with AUROC above 0.95. There is a $2.7M humanitarian analysis organisation that has publicly committed to expert judgement as its official method. And there is no product, anywhere, that captures expert judgement on a named humanitarian risk statement and gives the expert a receipt. That is the whole opportunity, and it is narrow, and it is real.

Five findings do most of the work. Each one changes something in the plan.

1

The exact product was proposed for this sector in January 2016, at Start Network, and it went nowhere.

A published concept note — Beating Hindsight: Forecasting for Humanitarian Planning and Preparedness — proposed a GJP-style humanitarian forecasting tournament managed by the Start Network: 4–5 staff from each member organisation, mixed field/regional/HQ, ~12 questions, 3-month rounds, 6–18 month horizons. It named the Early Warning Project as the closest existing analogue. It identified the real barrier correctly: "evidence provided by early warning projects has historically not led to improved response due to the lack of political will… and decisions are not evidence-based." Ten years later there is no such tournament.

Build implication: we are not pitching a novel idea, we are pitching a post-mortem. The pitch must open by naming the 2016 attempt and stating precisely why ours survives where that one died. That is a stronger opening than any demo, and it is unavailable to every other team in the room.
2

The closest analogue in the world lost 89% of its participants — and that is the design argument.

The Early Warning Project's expert opinion pool went from 10,825 votes in 2018 to 1,148 in 2021. Its own 2015 report already noted that a small share of participants accounted for most activity, and that engagement slowed in year two. This is the single most important number in this entire document, because it is measured on a well-funded, institutionally hosted, genuinely important elicitation exercise in exactly our domain.

Build implication: the receipt loop (E7) is not a delight feature. It is the documented survival condition of the product. Promote it from "stage 4" in the build order to co-equal with E3/E4, and make the pilot's primary success metric response retention at round 2, not accuracy.
3

ACAPS has already published "expert judgement" as its method. It just doesn't keep the receipt.

In its own methodology: "Risk probability is not mathematically calculated but assigned through expert judgement based on the triangulation of available data, historical patterns, forward indicators, and context knowledge." Impact is likewise "adjusted through expert judgement." And in a published Afghanistan risk report, the input survey went to more than 60 people and got 10 responses.

Build implication: stop describing the product as adding a method. Describe it as making ACAPS's own stated method auditable — and set an explicit, falsifiable target against their own published baseline: ≥50% response within 72 hours, versus a corpus where 73% of scored triggers have no written criterion to respond to.
4

Machine forecasting has won where things are countable, and still cannot answer ACAPS's actual question.

VIEWS posts AUROC ≈ 0.95 and Brier 0.0062 on conflict fatalities. WFP's HungerMap LIVE runs across 94 countries with 300+ analysts and gradient-boosted models. Neither produces a judgement on whether "escalating cross-border insecurity linked to the Sudan conflict increases protection risks in eastern Chad" should move from 40% to 55%. Meanwhile the machines that do forecast judgement-style questions have risen from below-crowd to near-expert: o3 scores Brier 0.135 against a Metaculus crowd of 0.149, still well above superforecasters at ~0.023 — with benchmark parity projected around August 2027.

Build implication: do not compete on prediction and do not put an LLM at the centre. Put the model in as the second baseline the expert is permitted to disagree with — which is already E3's design. The research supplies the exact numbers to defend it under questioning.
5

The commercial "why not just buy this?" question is real, has a name, and has a good answer.

Cultivate Labs / Hinsley is the closest commercial analogue: SOC 2 Type II, UK G-Cloud 14, built Cosmic Bazaar for the UK Cabinet Office and INFER for the US OSTP, 10M+ forecasts processed. It is also a generic tournament engine with no risk model, no trigger-level dual baselines, no anonymity tiers, no publication gate, and a scoring rule that has been formally shown to be not proper (relative-Brier gaming and Sybil vulnerability). And the RAND Forecasting Initiative — a well-funded institutional tournament built on that very platform — ran 2024 to 2026 and then shut down.

Build implication: answer the buy question in the pitch pre-emptively, in one line, with the RAND shutdown as evidence. A forecasting pilot without a named institutional consumer inside the publisher's workflow dies. Ours lands inside ACAPS's own Risk Radar update cycle.
02

The map: four layers, and where the hole is

The landscape is not a flat list of competitors. It is four layers, and each layer has a different relationship to our build. Confusing them is how pitches lose — a jury member who knows VIEWS will ask why we aren't using it; a jury member who knows Cultivate will ask why we aren't buying it. Both questions have answers only if the layers are kept separate.

LayerWhat it producesWho is in itRelationship to us
1 · The incumbent Human judgement on named humanitarian risk statements, published as a 5×5 probability/impact grid ACAPS Risk Radar Not a competitor — the customer. Their published methodology is the spec.
2 · The machines Statistical forecasts of countable quantities: fatalities, displacement, IPC phase, food consumption scores VIEWS, INFORM, WFP HungerMap LIVE, OCHA Centre, DRC Foresight, ICG eEARTH, Verisk Maplecroft Upstream suppliers, not rivals. They provide one of our two baselines and the honesty beat.
3 · The judgement platforms Aggregated probability from multiple people on resolvable questions Metaculus, Good Judgment, Cultivate/Hinsley, Hypermind, Swift Centre, EWP, CSET Foretell, RAND RFI dead The real competitive set. This is the "why not just buy/hire them" question.
4 · The harm-prevention stack Interventions at the point of online harm: warnings, detection, hashing, reporting, regulation Project Intercept, Thorn, Tech Coalition, INHOPE, IWF, national hotlines, EU CSAR UNICRI track only. Our concept sits between layers, which is its vulnerability.

The hole

Every layer is saturated except the seam between layer 1 and layer 3. Layer 1 has the domain but no elicitation infrastructure. Layer 3 has the elicitation infrastructure but no domain — no risk model, no trigger vocabulary, no country panels, no publisher. Nobody occupies that seam.

The positioning sentence the research earns you

Everyone either forecasts numbers or aggregates strangers. ACAPS needs judgement on its own sentences from its own people — and there is no product for that, because until now nobody had both the sentence bank and the panel.

03

Layer 2 — the machines

These are not competitors. They are the reason the brief says "ground truthing and forecasting" rather than just one. Read them for two things: what they prove is possible (credibility by association) and what they cannot do (the gap we occupy).

VIEWS — Violence & Impacts Early-Warning System

Threat: none
Uppsala University / PRIO · open source · monthly releases
Approach
Machine learning ensembles over a dozen-plus data providers and hundreds of features (conflict history, economy, governance, terrain, climate vulnerability), with an open MLOps pipeline.
Solution
Monthly probabilistic forecasts of state-based armed conflict, non-state conflict and one-sided violence, 1–36 months ahead, at country and subnational (PRIO-GRID) level. New forecasts released every month; the Oct 2026 release covers to Aug 2029.
Faring
The genuine state of the art. Country-month AUROC ≈ 0.95 and Brier ≈ 0.0062; correctly identified 7 of the 10 deadliest countries in 2024 and 6 of 10 in 2023, at a stated cost of ~35% false positives for 95% coverage. Free, funded by the Swedish Foundation for Strategic Research and partnered with UNHCR.
Borrow
The monthly cadence and the brutal habit of publishing coverage-versus-false-positive trade-offs rather than a single headline number.
Beat
Nothing. Do not compete. Instead, cite: "we are not claiming to beat the state of the art in conflict modelling; we are claiming to fix the step after it."

INFORM Risk & Severity

Threat: none — it is the vocabulary
EU Joint Research Centre · published twice yearly · API + Excel
Approach
Composite indicator index: hazard & exposure, vulnerability, coping capacity. ~54+ indicators for Risk, 40+ for Severity, aggregated to a 0–10 score and five classes.
Solution
Country-level structural risk (Risk) and crisis-level severity (Severity), heavily used across the humanitarian system and cited directly in ACAPS and IPC-adjacent products.
Faring
Ubiquitous and institutionally entrenched. It is the reference vocabulary: very low → very high, 0–10, twice-yearly.
Borrow
Nothing methodologically — borrow the legitimacy. Because ACAPS's Risk Radar already speaks INFORM's dialect, a product that speaks it too needs no explanation to a jury.
Beat
Its structural weakness, stated carefully: a composite index is a static ranking with no resolution criterion and no expert channel. It can say a country is high-risk; it cannot say whether a specific named event happened.

WFP HungerMap LIVE

Threat: medium — the scale benchmark
World Food Programme · 94 countries · relaunched April 2026 with Google support
Approach
Unified data lake (mVAM phone surveys, market prices, CHIRPS rainfall, MODIS vegetation, ACLED conflict, nightlights, macro indicators), with gradient-boosted tree ensembles (XGBoost) trained on 14 years across 63 countries, plus an LSTM predictive layer updating weekly.
Solution
Near-real-time nowcast plus short-term forecast of the prevalence of insufficient food consumption and crisis-level coping, at first-level administrative area. Public dashboard plus AI-assisted forecasts for 16 designated hunger hotspots.
Faring
The most institutionally successful product in this entire landscape. Validated against ex-post IPC classification with mean absolute deviation under 0.5 IPC phases in 82% of districts; WFP claims a minimum of $7 saved per $1 invested in anticipatory action. Draws on a network of 300+ analysts.
Borrow
The human-plus-machine framing honestly implemented — 300+ analysts and a model, not a model alone. And the return-on-investment framing for the pitch: $7 per $1 is a donor-facing number that travels.
Beat
The scope boundary. HungerMap answers "how many people are food insecure in this district." It cannot answer "was our risk statement right." It also proves the panel-size point in reverse: 300+ analysts is a data-collection workforce, not an elicitation panel.

OCHA Centre for Humanitarian Data — Predictive Analytics

Threat: medium — potential host or gatekeeper
UN OCHA · The Hague · Peer Review Framework · Catalogue of Predictive Models
Approach
An in-house team that develops, validates and peer-reviews predictive models, and supports CERF Anticipatory Action frameworks with triggers and thresholds; publishes a Catalogue of Predictive Models and a Peer Review Framework for Predictive Analytics in Humanitarian Response.
Solution
Technical support to country offices and to the two-stage CERF AA trigger architecture; sits alongside HDX, which it also manages.
Faring
Embedded and expanding — currently recruiting data scientists based in Nairobi to support AA frameworks and humanitarian response plan projections. The institutional centre of gravity for humanitarian predictive work.
Borrow
The Peer Review Framework. This is the closest thing the sector has to an accepted quality bar for a predictive model, and our product's validation story should be written to satisfy it.
Beat
Not a competitor, but a real risk: it is the obvious body to host a product like ours, which is both an opportunity (credibility) and a threat (why would ACAPS build rather than let OCHA do it). Answer: ACAPS owns the risk statements and the resolution record; that ownership is the asset.

DRC Foresight

Threat: none — the closest internal-deployment template
Danish Refugee Council · in deployment since early 2020 · 40 countries
Approach
Explainable machine learning (gradient-boosted trees per forecast horizon, plus structure-learned graphical models whose graph inputs were specified by subject-matter experts in workshops) for forced displacement, with a scenario layer.
Solution
Long-horizon displacement forecasts feeding DRC's annual Global Displacement Report, country strategic reviews, resource requirements and staffing decisions in Asia and West Africa and seven country operations.
Faring
One of very few genuinely deployed-and-used humanitarian forecasting systems — and notably, its authors report it "shifted organisational thinking around anticipative response."
Borrow
The deployment pattern: a forecast that lands in a recurring institutional document survives; a forecast that lands in a dashboard does not. This is the single most transferable finding in layer 2.
Beat
Nothing — but note the asymmetry. DRC's output is a number planners can use directly. ACAPS's output is a judgement about a sentence. That is precisely why DRC could automate and ACAPS cannot.

International Crisis Group — eEARTH

Threat: low — brand-adjacent
Crisis Group · Early Action & Risk Tracking Hub · CrisisWatch
Approach
"Fuses cutting-edge data analytics with political expertise" to detect resource-related conflict signals; backed by CrisisWatch's standing apparatus of ~70 monitored conflicts plus ~50 on standby monitoring.
Solution
An early-warning hub for climate-security and resource conflict, initially Horn of Africa, plus monthly conflict trend tracking.
Faring
New and credible on brand, but early — an initiative riding an existing reputation rather than an independently evaluated system.
Borrow
The standby monitoring concept: not every risk is live, but a named set is watched for drift. This maps cleanly onto ACAPS's active-vs-dormant risk split (108 of 139 risks carry triggers; only 76 sit on active risks).
Beat
N/A. But it is a reminder that "analytics plus expertise" is a crowded sentence, and we need a sharper one.

Verisk Maplecroft + IRC Emergency Watchlist

Threat: none for ACAPS; contrast only
Bath, UK · commercial indices · IRC annual product
Approach
Maplecroft: ~150+ commercial risk indices across 198 countries sold to corporates and investors (~$15M revenue, ~177 staff). IRC: an annual curated watchlist built from a transparent indicator set plus qualitative conflict analysis.
Solution
Country risk scoring for supply chain, human rights, ESG and civil unrest clients; IRC's watchlist for humanitarian prioritisation.
Faring
Maplecroft is commercially mature but is an index business, not a forecasting business — it also appears as an input in IRC's technical notes, which tells you where it sits in the value chain.
Borrow
Nothing directly. Useful as evidence that the buyer exists for risk products — just not this buyer.
Beat
N/A. Mention only if asked about commercial alternatives; the honest answer is that the commercial market solves a different customer's problem.
Do not say in the pitch

"We combine AI forecasting with expert input." Every layer-2 organisation already says that, with more data and bigger teams. The differentiating claim is narrower and harder: we are the only system that keeps the human judgement traceable to a named sentence and returns the outcome to the person who made it.

Layer 3 — the judgement platforms

This is the competitive set. One of these is the answer to "why not just buy it," one is the answer to "why not just hire them," and one is the answer to "has anyone tried this before."

Cultivate Labs / Hinsley

Threat: high
Chicago · founded 2012/2014 · ~18 staff · SOC 2 Type II · UK G-Cloud 14
Approach
Crowdsourced forecasting as a managed platform. Decompose and scenario, write resolvable questions, invite internal and external forecasters, collect rationales, aggregate, optionally layer an AI forecaster on top. Now marketed under the Hinsley brand as "AI-driven research, analysis and forecasting" with "continuous probabilistic foresight."
Solution
Cosmic Bazaar for the UK Cabinet Office; INFER for the US White House OSTP; Shell; and 10M+ forecasts processed for AbbVie, GE, LG and the US Intelligence Community.
Faring
Commercially the most successful entrant. SOC 2 Type II recertified annually, on the UK G-Cloud 14 framework — both of which matter enormously to a body like ACAPS, which is funded by ECHO, SDC and the Norwegian MFA and would need procurement cover. It also acquired the entire RAND Forecasting Initiative as a customer, and then lost it.
Borrow
The compliance posture. If the pilot ever becomes a procurement conversation, G-Cloud listing and SOC 2 are the artefacts that get it past a finance director.
Beat
Three specific, defensible gaps: (i) it is a generic tournament engine — no risk model, no trigger vocabulary, no dual baselines, no country panels; (ii) its scoring rule is not a proper scoring rule — published work shows relative-Brier scoring is gameable and Sybil-vulnerable; (iii) it is a platform, not a workflow, so it has no place to land inside the Risk Radar update cycle.

Good Judgment

Threat: medium
USA · commercial · network of Superforecasters
Approach
Recruit, train, team and aggregate: identify forecasting talent by track record, train cognitive debiasing, put forecasters in small teams, aggregate with extremising. Sells the output and the method as training and reports.
Solution
Probabilistic forecasts on client-specified questions, plus in-house forecasting capability building for governments and corporates.
Faring
The strongest public track record in the field: 831 resolved questions as of August 2026, with the highest-probability outcome correct on 73% of days; 79% of forecasts beat hybrid human-plus-machine teams; roughly 30% more accurate than intelligence analysts with classified access; beat all IARPA competitors by 35–72%; ~66% more accurate than futures markets on Fed calls. Anticipated events at 400 days out as accurately as ordinary forecasters at 150 days.
Borrow
The teaming effect (~10% of GJP's advantage came from recruitment and retention, and team structures add further gain) — which is why the panel should be a stable cohort, not a fresh anonymous crowd each round.
Beat
Four things, and this is the strongest answer in the deck: they are a paid vendor (a real budget line for a $2.7M organisation); their forecasters have no standing in the country being forecast; they produce forecasts, not revisions to published analysis; and they do not solve the problem in the brief, which is not accuracy but turning scattered expert knowledge into grounded analysis without costing experts their time or anonymity. Good Judgment optimises the forecast. ACAPS needs the loop.

Early Warning Project the cautionary case

Threat: low
US Holocaust Memorial Museum · opinion pool since 2013 · statistical risk assessment since 2015
Approach
Two mechanisms bolted together: a statistical risk assessment (SRA) producing country-level probabilities of mass-atrocity onset, and a public expert opinion pool in which participants forecast binary questions about specific countries.
Solution
An annual ranked watch list of countries at risk of mass killing, plus a public forecast tournament on atrocity onset.
Faring
Mixed, and instructive in both directions. The pool's aggregate Brier was 0.12 across 83 binary questions (0.09 for mass-killing onsets, 0.06 for state-perpetrated killing) — genuinely good, and achieved by an aggregate whose individual forecasters were barely better than random. The SRA reached AUROC 0.81 and its Top-30 captured 68% of onsets, but at 4% sensitivity it produced precision of roughly 5% — about twenty false positives per onset. And participation collapsed from 10,825 votes (2018) to 1,148 (2021).
Borrow
Two things. First, the proof that aggregating weak individuals yields strong signal — which is the single best argument for a 5–10 person panel drawn from non-superforecasters. Second, and more usefully, the decay curve as a design requirement.
Beat
Its failure mode. EWP asked experts to forecast events in countries they could not change, with no visible consequence, no feedback to the individual, and no institutional consumer. Every one of those four absences is a design decision we can reverse.
The number to put on a slide

10,825 → 1,148. An 89% collapse in participation, in a well-funded atrocity-forecasting pool, over three years. Any product in this space that does not answer this number with a mechanism is asking the jury to believe the same thing will not happen twice.

Metaculus

Threat: low
Public benefit corporation · ~28 staff · ~$8.8M raised · BSD-2 open source since 2024
Approach
Open forecasting platform with recency-weighted median aggregation, log scoring floored at 0.1%/99.9% to prevent certainty, and a two-dimensional Baseline/Peer scoring regime introduced November 2023.
Solution
~15,000+ questions and 1M+ predictions from a ~50,000-user community; API, tournaments, and a platform others can self-host since the 2024 open-sourcing.
Faring
Healthy as a platform, and honestly self-critical: Brier around 0.107 on 2021 questions; the aggregated "Metaculus Prediction" was deprecated in November 2024 in favour of the Community Prediction; their own Q2 2025 comparison showed Pro Forecasters still beating AI systems at p = 0.00001. Weaknesses are structural: many questions attract fewer than 50 forecasters, and calibration degrades on horizons beyond a year.
Borrow
The resolution criteria as contract discipline — no question opens until its death is specified. And the score floor: never permit 0% or 100%, because certainty is a failure of the instrument, not a statement about the world.
Beat
Everything about its social model. Public leaderboards, open crowds, and permanent profiles are the three features the ACAPS brief most directly forbids. Also: thin per-question liquidity means the aggregate on any single question is fragile — the opposite of a curated 5–10 person panel.

Swift Centre

Threat: low — but the best external validation we have
London · founded 2022 · ~2–10 staff · ~$2M Future Fund grant
Approach
A deliberately small elite panel plus published, resolved, individually-attributed public forecasts. Publishes its misses as prominently as its hits.
Solution
Public forecasts on geopolitical and policy questions, with an annual year-in-review that scores the whole set.
Faring
Aggregate Brier 0.115 across 13 public resolved forecasts in 2025 — competitive with much larger operations — and openly reports where it was wrong (LNG, Indus Waters) while noting where it beat Polymarket (Iran strike at 68% versus the market's 55%; Greenland at 4% versus 12%).
Borrow
This is the external evidence that a small panel with published receipts beats a large crowd. It is the single most citable proof-of-concept for BENCHMARK's architecture, produced by an organisation with no connection to the hackathon.
Beat
Nothing. Cite it. "A small panel that publishes its misses is competitive with much larger operations — that is our architecture, validated by someone else."

CSET Foretell · RAND Forecasting Initiative

Threat: none
Georgetown CSET · RAND · 2024–2026 shut down
Approach
CSET Foretell: a forecasting tournament testing whether crowd forecasts beat expert projections on emerging-technology indicators, with genuinely useful published question-design research. RAND RFI: a Cultivate-built platform on which several thousand analysts, SMEs and members of the public forecast geopolitical, technology and policy questions.
Solution
Both aimed to demonstrate that structured crowd judgement improves institutional analysis rather than to sell a product.
Faring
Foretell's headline result was real — crowd mean error 0.28 against 0.54 for historical projection, beating projection on 56% of metrics — but it remained an open proof of concept needing scale and, in its own framing, policymaker demand. RAND's initiative ran from 2024 to 2026 and then shut down, with the platform now a read-only archive of 298 scored questions.
Borrow
Question-design craft: a rolling multi-window format, explicit awareness that bucketing answers biases forecasters, and the finding that end users want a point estimate together with an 80% interval rather than a distribution.
Beat
Use their endings as the argument. Two institutional forecasting programmes with real funding and real prestige wound up within a few years. The lesson is not that forecasting doesn't work — it is that a forecasting programme without a permanent institutional consumer is a grant, not a product.

Hypermind · Social Science Prediction Platform

Threat: none
Hypermind since 2000 · SSPP academic consortium
Approach
Hypermind: three distinct products — a Champion Forecasting Panel of named elite forecasters, a crowd-forecasting polling platform, and a prediction market — sold as collective-intelligence tools. SSPP: a panel of academic forecasters predicting the results of untested social programmes.
Solution
High-end bespoke forecasting for corporates and agencies; and systematic ex-ante prediction of research findings at scale (2,500+ forecasters from 340+ institutions, 174 studies, 54 countries).
Faring
Hypermind is a long-lived boutique with a serious client list. SSPP is a genuinely functioning research instrument with 85,000+ predictions — proof that expert panels can be recruited and sustained when the panel has a reason to care about the answer.
Borrow
SSPP's answer to the retention problem: participants return because the resolution is intellectually interesting to them. That is a design lever for E7 — make the outcome of the risk the expert assessed genuinely informative, not just an administrative closure.
Beat
N/A.

What the methodology actually says

Risk probability is not mathematically calculated but assigned through expert judgement based on the triangulation of available data, historical patterns, forward indicators, and context knowledge. ACAPS Risk Methodology, as reproduced in published country risk reports

And on impact:

Impact level is also adjusted through expert judgement, informed by context knowledge and triangulated through qualitative and quantitative data, to account for a population's vulnerability to shocks, coping strategies, and local, national, and international response capacities. ACAPS Risk Methodology

Read together, these two sentences are the entire product case. ACAPS has publicly committed to expert judgement as its formal method, at two independent points in every risk — probability and impact — and then records only the resulting number. The judgement, the disagreement, the reasoning and the name all evaporate. There is no audit trail, no track record per expert, and no way for an expert to learn whether they were right.

The reframe this unlocks

BENCHMARK is not a new method imposed on ACAPS. It is ACAPS's own method, made auditable. That single sentence defuses the strongest objection a jury can raise — "this displaces our analysts" — because it replaces nothing. It records something the methodology already assumes happens.

Three details in the methodology that change the schema

Methodology detailWhat it meansChange to our plan
Updates every three months; past risks assessed and accuracy evaluated every six months There are two clocks, not one. Our blueprint assumed a single six-month cycle. Decouple them. Forecast horizon = 6 months (matching the accuracy review, and matching the brief). Prompt cadence = 3 months (matching the update cycle). Round 2 of the panel should land before the analyst's revision, not after.
Probability scale is very low / low / medium / high / very high. Impact scale is very low / low / moderate / significant / major. The two five-point scales use different words. Only the first two labels match. Do not reuse one label set for both. Any UI that shows "High impact" is wrong — ACAPS's impact scale says "significant" or "major." A jury of ACAPS analysts will notice this immediately, and it is a two-line fix that buys disproportionate credibility.
Risk level is a function of probability and impact; a low-probability risk stays in the analysis if impact is medium or above. Resolution is not one-dimensional. "Unresolvable" needs two-axis criteria. A risk can materialise in impact without the probability ever being tested, or vice versa. The resolver screen must allow "event occurred, but not with the stated impact," which is a different outcome from a clean miss.

The 17% response rate

This risk overview is the result of a workshop involving nine analysts and experts on Afghanistan and bilateral conversations with contextual experts. In addition, a short risk survey was sent out to more than 60 people, but the response rate was low with only 10 responses. ACAPS Afghanistan risk report, published methodology section

This is the closest thing ACAPS has published to a baseline for the exact behaviour we are trying to change. It is a survey — not the same instrument as our panel — but it is the only public evidence of what happens when ACAPS asks more than 60 external experts for a judgement. Ten replied.

It also sits alongside the fact that most ACAPS risk products are built from a workshop of 9 to 28 participants plus key-informant interviews, with the product's own limitations sections routinely noting that the analysis "draws on a small number of KIIs for a national-level product." ACAPS is already doing small-group elicitation. It just does it synchronously, in a workshop, with no anonymity, no independent first round, no scoring, and no feedback loop.

The honest counter-argument, and its answer

Someone will say: "workshops already work — we get 9 to 28 people in a room." True, and that is exactly the problem. A workshop is fast and social; it is also where the loudest analyst wins, where a junior national staff member cannot contradict a senior adviser, and where nothing survives except the minutes. The brief's own phrasing — without costing those experts their time or their anonymity — is a direct critique of the workshop. Our product is the asynchronous, anonymous, independent-first-round complement to a JAM, not a replacement for it.

06

The prior art nobody in the room will know about

In January 2016, Paul Currion published a concept note proposing exactly the thing we are proposing. It was never built. It is the most strategically valuable document in this entire research pass, and it should shape the first sixty seconds of the pitch.

Beating Hindsight: Forecasting for Humanitarian Planning and Preparedness

2016 · never implemented
Published 7 January 2016 · proposed host: Start Network
Proposed
A GJP-style forecasting tournament for the humanitarian sector: 4–5 national and international staff from each of 4–5 Start member organisations, "preferably a combination of field, regional and HQ staff," a three-month initial tournament including platform development and training, built on ~12 questions about displacement, damage, crisis duration and negotiation outcomes.
Horizon
Explicitly "6–18 months — the perfect scale for decision-making [in] the existing humanitarian programme cycle."
Named EWP
Correctly identified the Early Warning Project as the closest existing analogue — "a combination of statistical risk assessment and an expert opinion pool (i.e. a tournament approach)."
Diagnosed
Why early warning fails in this sector, in four lines that have not aged: "Evidence provided by early warning projects has historically not led to improved response due to the lack of political will… and decisions are not evidence-based." Plus: "the sector has a high barrier to entry that external experts find difficult to breach"; "the community struggles to incorporate new conceptual models into its workflows, since those workflows have been developed in an ad hoc and reactive manner"; and improved forecasts "will never predict the future with 100% certainty is likely to be off-putting to senior decision-makers."
Solved
Proposed the right remedy too: "experiment with ways of connecting forecasting directly to response, such as coordination agreements that set out clear actions once a forecast reaches a certain threshold."
Outcome
No tournament exists. Start Network did build FOREWARN (2016) — an expert network supporting anticipatory alerts — but as a technical advisory function for funding decisions, not as a scored, anonymised, feedback-looping panel. The forecasting ambition was absorbed into a different, narrower mechanism.

Why this matters more than any competitor

Every other team in the room will pitch this product as new. If it is new, the jury's implicit question is "will this work?" If it is ten years old and already failed once, the jury's question changes to "why will this work now?" — and that is a question we can answer, because we have read the post-mortem and the 2016 author had not yet seen the EWP decay curve, the RAND shutdown, or the LLM baseline.

What the methods literature actually establishes

Nine findings, each with a build consequence. This section is the ammunition for every methodological question in Q&A.

FindingEvidenceBuild consequence
Very small panels capture most of the gain Gaba et al. (2017) find most accuracy gains from as few as 5 independent estimates. EWP ran ~125 participants and decayed to irrelevance. Swift Centre operates with ~2–10 staff and scores Brier 0.115. The brief's 5–10 is correct and now evidenced three ways. Keep the hard schema cap. Do not let anyone "improve" it by opening the panel to hundreds — that is the EWP path.
Performance-weighted aggregation beats equal weights Cooke's Classical Model, developed at TU Delft and applied across 45 expert panels with problem-owner sign-off, weights experts by calibration on seed variables — and consistently outperforms equal weighting when seed data exists. In reliability settings, equal weighting plus a discussion-and-revision phase already improves calibration substantially. Do not weight by performance in v1. With 5–10 people and few resolved items per person, performance weights are noise. Use equal weights plus a revision round — which the reliability literature shows captures much of the benefit — and let A4 (panel health) display calibration without acting on it until n is large.
Extremising helps; over-engineering does not GJP's extremising step produced real gains. But our own ablation on 104 resolved ACAPS risks found mean 0.2196, median 0.2215, log-odds 0.2219 and extremised 0.2237 — differences inside noise. Ship equal-weight mean, describe extremising as available, and never claim the ablation as a discovery. Say plainly: "on our n, aggregation method does not matter; we chose the one a jury can audit."
"Surprisingly popular" is a real, published, large effect Prelec, Seung & McCoy (Nature, 2017): asking people what they think and what they think others will say, then selecting the answer that beats expectations, reduced error by 21.3% versus majority vote, 24.2% versus confidence-weighted vote, and 48% on the state-capitals set. This is a cheap, novel, defensible add. One extra field in E4 — "what will most other panellists say?" — and the analyst gets a divergence signal that is not just variance. It is the single most credentialled method in this table that no humanitarian product uses. Add it as an optional analyst-only view in stage 4.
Some scoring rules used in this field are provably not proper Published work (arXiv 2106.11248) shows the relative-Brier scoring regime used across GJ Open-style platforms is not a proper scoring rule — it is gameable via relative placement and vulnerable to Sybil attacks. Say this out loud in Q&A. "We use Brier and log score on the absolute outcome, not relative scoring, because relative scoring has been shown to be improper." It is a devastatingly specific answer to "how is this different from Metaculus/Cultivate."
Training produces ~10%, and the literature has been challenged GJP training improved Brier by roughly 10% across four years; recruitment and retention of better forecasters accounted for ~10% of GJP's advantage. But Hauenstein et al. (2025) argue the training effects may not be robust once question-set and timing differences are controlled. Promise a 90-second onboarding, not a training programme. Do not claim the 10% training effect as ours. Do claim the retention effect — because retention is the one GJP advantage that is not disputed, and it is exactly what E7 buys.
Attrition hits experts hardest In a long-run existential-risk forecasting tournament, dropout was 19% among superforecasters but 51% among domain experts — and the organisers reported real difficulty recruiting non-superforecasters willing to commit at all. Our panel is entirely domain experts. We are recruiting precisely the population with the worst demonstrated retention. This is the strongest argument that a 90-second round is a hard requirement, not a UX nicety, and that the pilot must instrument drop-off from invitation to submission.
Humanitarians systematically over-forecast An MSF analysis found roughly 70% of medical items were over-forecast, and ~40% of items had intermittent demand that breaks standard error measures. Judgemental forecasting is the norm in the sector, and it is biased in a predictable direction. A directional de-biasing cue is warranted. If humanitarian practitioners over-forecast, the analyst-side display should show panel mean against a base rate explicitly. And it gives a second reason for the model baseline: not to replace the panel but to pull against a known directional bias.
At the frontier, AI beats the crowd and trails the experts o3 scores Brier 0.1352 against a Metaculus crowd of 0.149 and superforecasters at ~0.0225 median on held-out questions. On ForecastBench in May 2026, superforecasters sit at ~0.086 and the best AI systems at ~0.103, with a median projection of full parity around August 2027. Calibration analysis shows LLMs are typically overconfident on high-probability events. Two consequences. (i) Never claim the LLM is the product — it is demonstrably still behind experts and the gap is closing on a timescale that will make any AI-centric moat worthless. (ii) The specific failure mode — overconfidence on high-probability events — is exactly the ACAPS pattern we measured (High risks materialise at 84%). A model baseline that is overconfident at the top is useful, because the divergence it creates is where the panel earns its keep.
The structural argument this section makes

Every methodologically serious choice in BENCHMARK turns out to be the choice the literature already recommends: small panel (Gaba), equal weights with a revision round (reliability literature), absolute proper scoring (arXiv 2106.11248), resolution criteria before opening (Metaculus's good habit), and a retention mechanism (GJP's undisputed advantage). We are not inventing a method. We are the first to apply an already-agreed method to risk statements rather than to standalone questions.

The UNICRI layer — revised

Revision note · 6 November 2026

This section previously described UNICRI as a track that "stays a second pod" on the strength of one paper. That judgement was made before the challenge repo was read file by file and before the nineteen primary sources behind it were worked through. It is withdrawn. The evidence base on this track is roughly ten times that of the ACAPS one, four randomised trials exist, and the field's central problem — that 70% of people who want help cannot find an anonymous route to it — is a routing problem rather than a persuasion problem. Project Intercept — Lucy Faithfull Foundation

Threat: the incumbent, but a consultancy rather than a product
UK charity · 22 bespoke interventions in 2 years · partners include OpenAI, Meta, TikTok, Google, Mega, Stability AI, Civitai, BT, Bing, Aylo, OnlyFans, MovieStarPlanet
Approach
Deterrence messaging at the moment of risk — on search terms indicating intent, or clicks to links previously reported as CSAM. Four message components derived from research with people who have offended: it is a crime · it causes harm · there are consequences · there is help.
Faring
70M+ warnings, at times 95,000 a day, in 131 countries; ~683,000 click-throughs, roughly 1%. Livingstone called that "disappointing" given the scale of the problem while noting four in five who do seek support engage with it. Note the denominator problem: each intervention is separately researched and built, roughly one per month, by a team of clinicians and engineers.
Borrow
The four-message structure, adopted verbatim rather than reinvented. And the honest denominator: Intercept publishes 70M and 700K in the same breath.
Beat
Not on distribution — that is structural. On economics. Intercept's model cannot reach a 20,000-member Dutch forum with one part-time moderator, and never will: the tail of platforms is long and each one is too small to justify bespoke R&D. Self-serve configuration is the entire opening.
What changed
The 2024 read was "we can beat them on segmentation." The revised read is sharper and less flattering to us: we can only beat them on installability, and only in the tail. Any pitch claiming to out-design a specialist charity with 22 deployments is not credible to a jury that contains one of its authors.

The detection layer — Shield, PhotoDNA, Cloudflare, IWF, Thorn

Threat: none — and this is the constraint, not the opportunity
Hashing, classification, blocking, reporting
Faring
Mature, funded, and free at the entry level. Cloudflare's CSAM Scanning Tool is on every plan including free, toggled from a dashboard with a verified email. Project Arachnid's Shield API is free with TS/Python/Rust/PHP SDKs. PhotoDNA is free for qualified organisations. IWF's Image Intercept is explicitly built for small businesses. Tech Coalition Pathways exists to onboard exactly the platform in the brief.
Borrow
The measurement discipline — count files, reports, latency; never sentiment.
Beat
Nothing. Concede this layer entirely. The strategic point is the inverse of competition: because detection is free and solved, a team that ships detection is shipping a solved problem. Seven free ways to find an illegal image, zero ways to interrupt the person looking. That sentence is the market gap, and it is stronger as a concession than as a claim.
What changed
The earlier read treated this layer as "the thing to sit above." The revised read is that it should be bundled — turned on by the same install — because the bundle is what makes adoption cheap, and none of the free tools currently reaches a small platform that has not already gone looking for them.

EU CSAR and DSA Article 28

Threat: a tailwind, not a customer — and not law
Proposed 11 May 2022 · Council position Nov 2025 · fifth trilogue failed June 2026 · ePrivacy derogation extended to 3 April 2028 (Reg. 2026/1881) · sixth trilogue scheduled
Approach
Risk assessment and proportionate mitigation obligations on hosting and interpersonal-communication providers, layered on DSA art. 28; Council's 2025 position dropped mandatory detection orders in favour of risk-based mitigation and voluntary detection; a 45M MAU threshold for the heaviest duties.
Borrow
The risk-categorisation logic — obligations scale with assessed risk, not only with platform size. That is a legitimate pattern for an intervention that cannot show everyone the same interstitial.
Beat
Do not build against it. But do note the direction of travel in one sentence: risk-mitigation-by-design is the only mechanism by which the tail of platforms will ever be compelled to install anything, and it is the difference between a voluntary module and a mandated one.
What changed
Unchanged as a caution. It was and remains a reason to build a voluntary product rather than a compliance product.

The help layer — Offlimits, ReDirection, Kein Täter werden, Stop It Now UK

Threat: none. This is the actual bottleneck.
The organisations a module hands people to
Faring
Small and fragile. The Dutch prevention line took 943 contacts in an entire year (2022) — about 2.6 a day for 17.9 million people. Insoll's survey of 4,493 CSAM users found 55% want to stop and 70.2% could not find an anonymous route. Nurmi's Tor study found 73.9% of help-seekers could not get help. Karolinska's Prevent It 2.0 shows what happens when they do: urges fall by d = −1.01, CSAM viewing by 1.87 h/week.
Borrow
The routing table. Country, language, service type, confidentiality terms, opening hours, capacity.
Beat
Nothing. This layer is the customer's last mile, not a competitor. But it carries the single biggest delivery risk in the whole product — see the capacity callout below.
What changed
This layer was absent from the previous version of this section and it is the most important one. The literature is unambiguous: treatment works and routing fails. A product that improves routing is doing the highest-value work available and the easiest to defend.
The capacity contradiction — the one thing that could sink the whole design

If a module installed across a few hundred Dutch platforms worked at even Project Intercept's rate, it would generate help-seeking demand far above 2.6 contacts a day. Offlimits is a small team. Success breaks the last mile. Any credible design therefore has to default to self-help (Stoppen is mogelijk, ReDirection) rather than the phone line, reserve human contact for signals of higher need, and give the service a volume forecast it can staff against. A design that routes everyone to a helpline is not ambitious — it is irresponsible, and a juror from the sector will say so.

Revised strategic call

The UNICRI claim still cannot be falsified offline in a weekend — there is no equivalent of 104 resolved risks, and that asymmetry is real and permanent. But the earlier conclusion drawn from it — "build the measurement harness, not the intervention" — was wrong, because the field's binding constraint is not measurement. It is that seven in ten people who want help cannot find the door, and no installable artefact exists that opens it.

The correct shape is therefore an artifact plus an honest measurement design: a self-serve module that installs in an afternoon, collects aggregate counts that cannot identify anyone, and ships with a pre-registered protocol for testing it properly. That is defensible, shippable in a weekend, and answers the adoptability criterion rather than dodging it.

New questions for ACAPS

  • "You've published a formal methodology with two five-point scales that use different words for impact and probability. Should the panel see both scales, or only the risk level?" — signals we read the method, not just the brief.
  • "Has anyone proposed a forecasting tournament to ACAPS before? What happened?" — if they know about 2016, we look rigorous; if they don't, we have handed them something valuable.

What I did not find, and would check next
