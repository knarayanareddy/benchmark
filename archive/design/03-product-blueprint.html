01 — The promise, and the posture

BENCHMARK asks seven people who know a country to spend ninety seconds each telling ACAPS where it is wrong — and then it goes to the trouble of proving whether they were right.

That is the whole product. Everything below is in service of two sentences a panelist should be able to say after their first session:

"That took me a minute and a half, and nobody will ever know it was me."

"Six weeks later they told me what happened, and my dissent changed the paragraph."

The posture this dictates

The product must feel like a considered instrument, not an app. The people using it are country directors, field MEAL officers and conflict researchers. They have been asked to join a hundred platforms and abandoned all of them. The way to earn their attention is not delight — it is demonstrable restraint.

Where most products signal quality with features, this one signals it by doing less in view. No onboarding carousel. No progress ring. No badge. No "Welcome back, Alex! 👋" — the panelist is anonymous by default, so the product cannot even greet them by name unless they chose the tier that allows it.

02 — Seven design laws

#LawWhat it means in practice
1Blindness before judgementA panelist cannot see any other number before committing their own. Enforced in the data model, not the interface — round 1 forecasts are rejected by the server if a group display was rendered for that user. Independence is a property of the system.
2Every question states how it diesThe resolution criterion and the date are visible before the forecast, in the same breath as the question. You cannot forecast honestly without knowing how you will be judged.
3Show the uncertainty, not the estimateNever display a single number where a range belongs. ACAPS says 20% — the product also says risks rated Low have historically materialised 43% of the time. The expert sees the honest picture.
4Holding is as respectable as movingIn round 2, keeping your answer and changing it get identical visual weight. Convergence must never be rewarded by design.
5Refusal is a featureWhen the system will not publish something, it says so, explains why, and offers a route. A product that visibly declines to act is more trustworthy than one that always complies.
6No metric rewards presenceThere is no streak, no count of visits, no "you're in the top 10% of contributors". The only score is accuracy, and it is private.
7Close the loop or don't open itNo contribution is ever accepted without a commitment to come back to that person with what happened. If the resolution queue is not staffed, the question does not open.

04 — The expert journey

Seven screens. Each with the actual copy, and the reason it is written that way.

E1

First contact — the invitation

once · 40 seconds
On screen

Eastern Chad panel

Alexander at ACAPS invited you. Seven people who know eastern Chad are being asked the same three questions about a risk ACAPS published in October.

Each takes about ninety seconds. Answer as many as you want. Stop at any point.

Nothing you say is published with your name. You will choose how anonymous you are on the next screen — before you tell us anything.

Why this copy: it names a real person, states the panel size, gives the exact time cost, grants permission to stop, and puts the anonymity guarantee before any data capture. "Before you tell us anything" is the trust hook — it proves the ordering is real, not a settings page buried later.

E2

Anonymity — chosen first, not last

once · changeable anytime
On screen

How anonymous do you want to be?

Known to ACAPS
ACAPS can see this came from you. Nobody else, ever. We can follow up, and we can credit you inside ACAPS.

Pseudonymous to peers
ACAPS knows it's you. Other panelists see "Expert B". The public sees nothing. We can ask you a follow-up without naming you.

Fully anonymous
ACAPS does not know this came from you — the link between you and your answer is destroyed on receipt. We cannot follow up, and we cannot credit you. That is the trade.

You can change this at any time. Changing it applies to everything not yet published.

Why this works: three options with concrete consequences, including the cost of the most private one. Saying "we cannot follow up" out loud is what makes the promise credible — a tier that only lists benefits reads like marketing. The changeable-anytime line removes the fear of choosing wrong.

Ground rule satisfied: "Each expert chooses their own level of anonymity (for example: known to ACAPS, pseudonymous to peers, or fully anonymous)."

E3

The question — with both baselines visible

per question · 90 seconds
On screen

Question 2 of 3 · eastern Chad

Will attacks on strategic infrastructure in Chad occur before 30 April 2027?

This is trigger T0619 of risk R1043 — "Escalating cross-border insecurity linked to the Sudan conflict increases protection risks and humanitarian needs in eastern Chad."

ACAPS currently estimates 40%.
Risks rated this level have materialised 84% of the time historically.

Resolves 30 April 2027.
Yes, if at least two independent sources report an attack on strategic infrastructure in Chad between now and then. Read the full rule

What do you think?

Tap to start near your view, then adjust. Most people stop after one tap.

The two baselines

ACAPS's probability rating for the parent risk (84% historical materialisation at High) and the trigger's own completion rate (40%) are both shown. Where they diverge by more than 20 points — as here — the product asks one extra question (E4). This is the feature that comes from parsing all 266 trigger blocks, and no other team will have it.

The input

Two-step: tap a coarse band, optionally nudge. Fast path is one tap; precise path is three. No slider, no pre-filled default, no anchoring thumb. The five ACAPS labels appear as reference marks on the scale so the expert can see exactly where their number sits against ACAPS's own vocabulary.

Why the honest base rate: showing "ACAPS says 20%" alone would be a point estimate masquerading as certainty. Showing and here is how risks at that level actually behaved is the difference between a survey and an instrument — and it is computed directly from the 104 resolved risks in the export.

Ground rule satisfied: "How can an expert contribute something valuable in a few minutes, at the moment it matters?" — ninety seconds, one screen, no scrolling required on a phone.

E4

The divergence moment — the signature interaction

conditional · 20 seconds
On screen — appears only when the two baselines disagree by >20 points

You said 72%.

ACAPS rates this risk High overall — but the specific trigger sits at 40%.

Which is closer to what you're seeing?

The risk as a whole is worse than the trigger suggests
The trigger is the real story — the risk rating is stale
Both, for different reasons
Skip

Why this is the product's best idea: the divergence between a risk-level rating and its trigger-level completion rate is a genuine signal, computed from data almost nobody will parse. Putting it in front of an expert as a single four-way question converts a subtle analytic insight into twenty seconds of work — and produces a structured object ACAPS has never had: where the risk model and the trigger model disagree, and which one practitioners trust.

Restraint: it only appears when the baselines genuinely conflict. Conditional, rare, meaningful. A product that asked this every time would be exhausting; one that asks it once every four questions feels like it is paying attention.

E5

The reason — optional, one line

per question · 15 seconds
On screen

One sentence on why?

Optional. Skip it if you'd rather not — but a line here is what turns a number into something an analyst can use.

[ text field, one line, 140 characters ]

Skip · Done

Why optional matters: making the reason mandatory would double the completion time and lose a third of the panel. Making it optional but honestly valuable gets you reasons from the people who have them — and those are the reasons worth having. The 140-character cap forces the discipline that makes a reason usable.

E6

The group view — round 2

48 hours later · 60 seconds
On screen

Here's where the panel landed.

Six of seven have answered.

[ distribution band: 15% ● · 55% · 60% · 62% · 68% · 72% · 80% ]

3 of 7 above 60% · 1 well below · spread 65 points

You said 72%.

Reasons the panel gave:
"Road access to the east has been cut twice since June — the infrastructure target is no longer theoretical."
"I'd disagree with the group here. The attacks so far have been symbolic, not strategic."

Change your answer, or hold it?

Change · Hold at 72%

Why the dissent is visible and named as dissent: the second reason is the most valuable object on the screen — an expert explicitly disagreeing with the emerging consensus. Most products would bury it as "comment 2". Here it is the point.

Why "Hold" is a real button: if changing your mind is a button and keeping it is a back arrow, the interface is telling people to conform. Equal weight is a design commitment to Law 4 — and it is measurable, because the product records how many people held under pressure. That number is the answer to the brief's groupthink question.

Ground rule satisfied: "Experts respond independently, see an anonymised summary of the group, and can refine their view in the next round" — Delphi, literally, and the anonymity holds because no individual is ever attributed to a position.

E7

The receipt — why anyone comes back

weeks or months later · 30 seconds
On screen — the retention moment, and the whole brief in one screen

Your call resolved.

T0619 — attacks on strategic infrastructure in Chad — did not happen before its deadline.

You said 72%. The panel said 61%. ACAPS said 40%.
You were 5 points off. The panel was 10. ACAPS was 21.

What changed because of your dissent:
Your round-2 note — "the attacks so far have been symbolic, not strategic" — went into the R1043 rationale. Alexander rewrote the paragraph on 12 December. Here it is.

[ the before-and-after paragraph ]

Your calibration: 84th percentile across 12 resolved questions.
Visible only to you. We never rank people publicly.

Why this is the single most important screen: the brief states the failure mode explicitly — comparable forecasting pools lose participation after launch. The cause is never the interface; it is that contributors never learn whether they mattered. This screen is the answer, and it is cheap to build.

Why the percentile is private: the brief lists a super-estimator scoreboard as a could. A public ranking of named experts would destroy the anonymity model and turn a trusted panel into a leaderboard. A private percentile gives the same recognition with none of the exposure.

Note what it admits: "you were 5 points off." The product does not flatter. A receipt that only ever says "great call!" is not evidence — it is a notification.

05 — The analyst journey

Desktop. Twenty minutes a week. The rule is that the moderator sees only exceptions — everything routine is automatic.

A1

The exception queue

weekly · 20 min budget
On screen

3 things need you. Everything else is handled.

① Strong dissent — Chad, T0619
1 of 7, fully anonymous, answered 15% against a panel of 61%. Reason attached. This is the highest-value item in the queue.

② Resolution due — Chad, T0618
Deadline in 4 days. Two candidate sources already linked.

③ Publication blocked — Chad, country aggregate
The gate refused. Reason available.

See all 6 panels ›

Why an exception queue and not a dashboard: a dashboard invites browsing; a queue invites finishing. The brief asks how ACAPS moderates "without adding a heavy workload" — the answer is that the product decides what deserves a human and presents only that. Twenty minutes a week is a target with a measurement behind it, not a hope.

Note the ordering: the dissent is item ①, above the operational tasks. The product is opinionated about what matters.

A2

Cited synthesis

per cycle · 10 min
On screen — AI behind the glass, every sentence traceable

Draft rationale — R1043, updated cycle

The panel was materially more pessimistic than the published rating on cross-border attacks [1], though one anonymous expert dissented sharply [2]. Reported road closures since June were cited as the main driver [3].

[1] 5 of 7 forecasts · [2] contribution #14 · [3] contribution #9

Not generated: claims about Chadian military movements — no panelist raised it and no source is attached. Write this yourself or leave it out.

Approve · Edit · Reject

Why every sentence carries a citation: one uncited generated sentence, spotted by an analyst who knows the domain, and the whole system loses credibility. The refusal line at the bottom is more valuable than the draft itself — it shows the model declining to invent in front of the person whose job is to check.

Ground rule satisfied: "AI support behind the scenes that helps ACAPS moderators synthesise expert input, as long as the experience for experts stays human." This is the only place AI appears, and the expert never sees it.

A3

Resolution — the promise kept

at the horizon · 5 min
On screen

Resolve T0619 — due 30 April 2027

Criterion: at least two independent sources report an attack on strategic infrastructure in Chad between 1 Nov 2026 and 30 Apr 2027.

Evidence attached: 2 sources · add another

Verdict: Fired · Did not fire · Unresolvable

Second resolver required before this closes.

Why "unresolvable" is a first-class option: pretending every question resolves cleanly is how forecasting systems rot. Logging a question as unresolvable is a design failure with a name — it gets counted, and it changes the templates.

Why two resolvers: a single person deciding whether a forecast was right turns scoring into one person's judgement. Two, with a documented tie-break, makes the track record defensible.

A4

Panel health

monthly · 2 min
On screen

Chad · 7 of 10 places filled

Median completion 1 m 22 s · week-8 retention 86% · moderator load 18 min/week · 0 anonymity incidents

⚠ One number to watch: 4 of 7 held their round-1 answer in round 2. If that stays high, the panel is anchored rather than deliberating — see the protocol note.

Why "held" is a warning sign, not a success: if everyone holds, the round-2 discussion added nothing and the panel is anchored on round 1. If everyone moves, they are being swept by the group. The healthy band is in between, and the product watches for it.

Why moderator minutes are on screen: the adoption constraint is stated and measured in the same place, in front of the person who has to live with it.

06 — The public page

The brief asks that outcomes be shared publicly. This is the accountability layer — and the only place a stranger meets the product.

P1

Resolved questions and verdicts

continuous
On screen

What we predicted, and what happened

Every question this panel has resolved, including the ones they got wrong.

T0619 — attacks on strategic infrastructure, Chad
Panel 61% · ACAPS 40% · Did not fire · 30 Apr 2027

T0618 — cross-border attacks, Chad
Panel 78% · ACAPS 40% · Fired · 3 Apr 2027

Panel calibration to date: 92% of forecasts within ±15 points · 14 resolved · 3 wrong

No individual expert is ever named or identifiable on this page. Panel sizes are small by design; where publishing a breakdown could expose a member, we publish less.

Why the wrong ones are listed: a public scoreboard that only shows wins is a marketing page. The credibility of the whole instrument comes from publishing the misses alongside the hits — and it costs nothing, because the data is already there.

Ground rule satisfied: "outcomes shared publicly" and "individual contributions are always anonymous to the public," in the same screen, with the tension between them acknowledged rather than hidden.

07 — The gate

The single behaviour that no other team will have, and the one you should demo first.

G1

Publish refused

whenever an aggregate would expose someone
On screen — analyst surface, red state

Cannot publish this view.

2 experts in this cell, one of them fully anonymous. A reader who knows which two people work on eastern Chad could work out which position was whose by elimination.

Three routes:

① Wait — a third contributor makes the cell safe (currently 2 of 3 needed)
② Publish the country-level aggregate instead
③ Widen the date range so the cell is no longer distinctive

Why you're seeing this: we check every publication against small-cell, differencing and attribute-combination risk before it goes out. How the check works

Why refusal beats a policy document: every submission will claim anonymity. Almost none will be able to show it. Ten seconds of the system declining to publish — with a plain-language reason — is worth more than a page of threat-model prose, and it is unforgettable in a room of judges who have seen forty dashboards.

Why three routes and not just "blocked": a gate that only says no is a nuisance. A gate that says no and offers a safe alternative is a colleague. This is also what makes it usable by ACAPS after the pilot — the moderator needs a way forward, not a wall.

Ground rule satisfied: "guarantee that nothing is attributed publicly" — turned from a promise into an enforced precondition.

08 — Ground rules compliance

Every rule in the ACAPS brief, mapped to the specific product behaviour that satisfies it. No rule left to policy.

RuleBrief languageProduct behaviour
MUST"Be easy to use and feel like an interesting community and experience that experts want to come back to"Seven screens, 90-second budget, no gamification. Returning is driven by E7 (the receipt), not by notifications.
MUST"Each expert chooses their own level of anonymity"E2, presented before any data capture, changeable at any time, with retroactive effect on unpublished output.
MUST"Individual contributions are always anonymous to the public"The gate (G1) plus the public page's explicit statement of what it withholds.
SHOULD"Maintain deep engagement over time while respecting the experts' time"Event-driven prompts only — a prompt fires when a risk analysis is published or a trigger moves, never on a calendar.
SHOULD"Short, focused contributions rather than long sessions"One question per screen. Median completion is measured and published internally against the 90-second target.
SHOULD"Fit a known, trusted group moderated by ACAPS, deliberately kept small (5–10 per country)"Hard schema constraint: the panel table rejects an 11th member. Recruitment is ACAPS's, never the product's.
SHOULD NOT"Focus solely on time spent in the app"No session-length metric exists. The north star is documented changes to published analysis. Time-in-app is deliberately not tracked.
SHOULD NOT"Feel like an AI chatbot"Zero AI in the expert surface. Not one generated word reaches a panelist. AI exists only in A2.
COULD"Scoreboard of super estimators"Private percentile in E7 only. No public ranking of named experts, ever.
COULD"Show where experts agree and disagree, including by geography"E6 shows spread and names dissent as dissent; the moderator view disaggregates by in-country vs. outside.
COULD"Work on mobile and low bandwidth"PWA, offline queue, a light mode that strips everything but text, payload budget enforced in CI.
COULD"Support French and other working languages of field experts"French ships with the Chad pilot, chosen at E1, not buried in settings. Arabic and Spanish at scale.
CAN"AI support behind the scenes for moderators"A2, citation-mandatory, explicitly refuses to generate unsupported claims.
DATA"Do not use personal data of real experts during the hackathon — work with synthetic users and example contributions"All demo personas synthetic. Real experts appear only in the research study (E3), with informed consent and no personal data stored.
DELIVER"A working prototype, a 3-minute pitch, a repository link and a short description"The repo contains the benchmark script, the red-team suite and a README that states what works and what does not. The pitch is written to 2:45 with five Q&A answers prepared.

09 — Nine things it must never do

Ground rules tell you what to build. This list tells you what would quietly break the trust the rules exist to protect.

1. Show a panelist another panelist's number before they have committed their own. Independence is enforced in the data model, not the interface.
2. Publish an aggregate that could let a reader identify a contributor — even if the aggregate is convenient, even if the moderator asks twice.
3. Put a single generated sentence into an analyst draft without a citation. The refusal is the feature.
4. Rank named experts, publicly or to peers. Percentiles are private, always.
5. Ask for more than a few minutes in one sitting, or open a question whose resolution is not staffed.
6. Count engagement as success. No streaks, no badges, no "you're in the top 10% of contributors."
7. Reward convergence. Holding and moving carry identical weight, and the split is monitored for anchoring.
8. Claim anonymity it cannot deliver. The threat model is scoped in the product and names what it does not defend against.
9. Let a resolved question go unreported to the person who forecast it. No receipt, no right to have asked.

10 — The visual system

Type and density

One family, two weights. Body 16–17px on mobile with generous leading — experts read this in a gap between meetings, often outdoors. Numbers are tabular and monospace, because figures that shift horizontally look untrustworthy. Headings are small and quiet; the number is the only thing allowed to be large on a screen.

Colour

Near-monochrome. One accent (a deep red) reserved for exactly three things: the dissent, the refusal, and a miss. Green appears only for a resolved-and-correct outcome. Risk levels use a warm-to-cool ramp matched to ACAPS's own conventions so the product feels native to their world, not ours.

Motion

Almost none. One transition: when a distribution appears after you submit, the band settles over 400ms — enough to read as a response, not enough to be a performance. No confetti, no celebration, no haptics beyond a single confirm tap.

Language

Plain, specific, unhedged. No exclamation marks. No emoji. No "Let's get started!" Written like a competent colleague who respects your time: short sentences, concrete nouns, numbers where numbers exist. French is a first-class language, not a translated afterthought.

The feel in one line: it should look like something a research institute built for its own use, not something a startup built to impress a jury. That is precisely why it will impress the jury.

11 — The five-minute demo

The UNICRI pitch guide states the format: 3 minutes of pitch, then 2 minutes of Q&A. Assume ACAPS runs the same. Build both halves.

The 3-minute pitch, with its visual beats

0:00 — The finding. Screen: the calibration table.
"ACAPS rates a risk Low. In ACAPS's own resolved history, Low risks materialised 43% of the time. Their analysis is good — and it is systematically under-confident at exactly the moment it matters most. Before we asked anyone for four minutes, we measured the number an expert panel has to beat."

0:40 — The bar. Screen: Brier comparison.
"A logistic regression on ACAPS's own structured fields reaches 0.218 with no human input. ACAPS's own analysts reach 0.217. Here is our panel's number, with confidence intervals — and if it does not beat the analysts, we will tell you that too."

1:05 — The demo. Live, on a phone, mirrored.
"Chad. One trigger, two baselines shown honestly. Ninety seconds. The panel lands at 61%, ACAPS at 40% — and one anonymous expert says 15% and explains why."

1:50 — The gate. The refusal.
"Now the part we are proudest of. We try to publish the sub-national view. The system refuses, and tells us why: with two experts in this cell, one anonymous, the position is identifiable by elimination. Three routes forward, one of them safe today."

2:20 — The receipt. E7.
"Six weeks later the question resolves. She was wrong — and the product tells her so, with her score, and with the note that her dissent changed the published paragraph anyway. That is the entire retention strategy, and it is why the panel is still there in December."

2:45 — The ask.
"Chad, seven experts, two cycles, eight weeks. The analyst material is already in place. Say yes on Monday and it starts Monday."

The five Q&A answers to have ready

Likely questionThe answer, in one breath
"How do you know the panel beats ACAPS rather than just agreeing with it?"That is exactly why we built the baseline first. An expert who copies ACAPS scores zero skill by construction — the scoring is relative to their number, not to 50%.
"What stops an expert ignoring the prompt?"Nothing, and that is correct — the panel is seven people, not a market. The design answer is the receipt: people return for evidence that they mattered, not for reminders. If retention drops below 50% by week four we stop and diagnose before adding a second country.
"How does this work across countries and languages?"Panels are per-country and independent, 5–10 each. French ships with Chad. The anonymity gate is per-panel, so a small country panel simply publishes less — the model degrades safely, it does not fail.
"What data does ACAPS have to share?"The Risk Radar export, which they already publish, plus a resolution decision when a question closes. Everything else is derived. No personal data about experts leaves their systems.
"Isn't this just a survey?"A survey has no resolution date, no baseline, and no consequence. Every question here states how it dies, is scored against what ACAPS already believed, and returns to the person who answered it. That is an instrument.

12 — Build pathway in order

The order matters more than the list. Each stage produces the thing the next stage depends on.

STAGE 1
The number

Benchmark script. Base rate, ACAPS probability, structured-field model, cross-validated. Nothing else can be designed until this exists.

STAGE 2
The parser

Full 266-block parse: completion rate, trend, dates, history. This is the moat and it unlocks E3, E4 and A4.

STAGE 3
E3 + E4

The question screen with both baselines, and the divergence prompt. The expert-facing core.

STAGE 4
E6 + E7

Group view and receipt. The two screens that make it a loop instead of a form.

STAGE 5
G1

The gate and its refusal. Build the red-team suite first, then the engine that passes it.
